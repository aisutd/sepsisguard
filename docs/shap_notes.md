The features that emerged as most important for predicting sepsis in our baseline model were ICULOS, Magnesium, and WBC. It was somewhat surprising to see Magnesium rank so highly alongside ICULOS, as clinical intuition often points immediately to vital signs such as temperature or heart rate. However, ICULOS (length of ICU stay) ranking among the top features makes clinical sense because longer ICU stays may indicate greater underlying illness severity and increased exposure to potential complications.

The baseline model's SHAP analysis provides another perspective, indicating that features such as ICULOS, Temp, Resp, and HR frequently dominate the feature importance rankings for individual patient predictions. Vitals such as respiratory rate and heart rate are clinically meaningful because acute changes in breathing patterns and hemodynamics can indicate physiological stress and potential sepsis. Additionally, lactate levels can reflect tissue hypoperfusion, while body temperature changes may indicate an infectious response. Together, these findings suggest that the model considers both laboratory measurements and physiological indicators when generating sepsis risk predictions.

Comparing the 6-hour and 12-hour prediction horizons reveals that ICULOS remains the top-ranking baseline factor, with its absolute SHAP magnitude increasing from 0.0174 to 0.0334 at the longer prediction horizon. Physiological indicators such as RESP and HR also demonstrate greater importance in the 12-hour model, highlighting the contribution of cardiorespiratory patterns to longer-term risk predictions. While Temp drops out of the top five features at 12 hours, SBP (systolic blood pressure) emerges in its place, suggesting a greater contribution from hemodynamic indicators. Meanwhile, Lactate maintains a consistent presence across both prediction windows, with its SHAP magnitude increasing slightly to 0.0038. These changes suggest that longer-horizon predictions may rely more heavily on broader physiological patterns rather than immediate changes in individual vital signs.

Maritza's trend-based analysis further complements these findings. Temporal changes and fluctuations in vital signs, particularly temperature and respiration, appear among the important features. Unlike individual measurements, these trend features capture how a patient's condition evolves over time, potentially identifying early signs of clinical deterioration.

Overall, the findings suggest that sepsis prediction benefits from combining patient-level context, laboratory measurements, physiological indicators, and temporal trends. Although feature rankings vary across prediction horizons and model configurations, ICULOS consistently emerges as an influential predictor. Importantly, SHAP values explain how individual features contribute to model predictions; they represent model-based feature attributions rather than direct causal relationships. These findings provide a clinically interpretable foundation for understanding the model's sepsis risk predictions.
